02

Your voice, all the way there

Saying "hello" moves a diaphragm by a fraction of a micron. Twelve transformations later it moves someone else's eardrum, hundreds of kilometres away, about a tenth of a second later. Press play.

Why your phone carries so many radios

One number decides almost everything about a mobile network: the carrier frequency. Low frequencies travel far and pass through walls but carry little. High frequencies carry enormous amounts and stop at a window pane. Drag the slider.

    05

    How the glass feels your finger

    Under the glass is a grid of transparent electrodes. Where your finger comes close it steals a tiny amount of charge — femtofarads — from the crossing below it. The controller reads the whole grid, finds the bump, and hands the operating system a single pair of coordinates. Move your pointer over the panel.

    Move over the panel · press and hold for a firmer press · touch works too

    What the controller sees

    View

    Real panels scan mutual capacitance at every crossing 60–240 times a second. The controller rejects a palm, interpolates the peak to far finer than the 4–5 mm electrode pitch, and only then reports a touch.

    06

    One chip, a dozen specialists

    Almost everything is on a single piece of silicon the size of a fingernail. It is not one processor but a committee of them, and the trick is that most are asleep most of the time. Pick a workload and watch who wakes up.

    07

    From your tap to the transistor

    A tap on a button crosses seven or eight layers of software before anything physical happens — and one of those layers runs on a processor your operating system cannot see. Click a layer.

    The short version

    It is a radio first

    Everything else is decoration on top of a machine whose job is to turn bits into a modulated wave and back, thousands of times a second, while moving.

    It is mostly asleep

    The reason a phone lasts a day is that its blocks race to finish and then power down. Average draw is measured in tens of milliwatts, not watts.

    The network does the hard part

    Your phone is one end of a scheduled conversation. The tower decides, millisecond by millisecond, who transmits, when, on which subcarriers, and how densely.

    Two computers, one box

    The applications processor runs your OS. The baseband runs its own real-time OS, its own firmware, and its own timing — and the two only exchange messages.
